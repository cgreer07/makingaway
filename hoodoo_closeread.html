Making A Way

Understanding the Development of Hoodoo Cultural Traditions in the United States

A visual journey through the African regions, forced migrations, and cultural traditions that shaped Hoodoo in North America.

Welcome

It’s no mistake that you have arrived here today. If you’re anything like me, you’re here because you’ve already heard the whispers… and now you’re curious.

For many of us, there is a second coming of age that involves unlearning old worldviews, learning a new perspective, and recontextualizing your identity. So, allow me to introduce you to the often misunderstood tradition called Hoodoo.

Hoodoo, also known as “conjure,” is a spiritual folk tradition created by enslaved African Americans in the southern United States that is heavily influenced by West and West Central African religions, Native American herb and healing traditions, as well as European influences and Christianity.

It isn’t a religion in an institutional sense, but it is a system of practical knowledge passed down and adapted across generations.

This is a story about making a way, persevering through every system built to strip away autonomy, agency, and expression, and building a culture that survived anyway.

African Origins

The people forced across the Atlantic came from dozens of distinct ethnic groups — each with its own language, cosmology, and way of understanding the sacred.

Yoruba

Among West Africa’s many peoples, the Yoruba (5) stand out as one of the most significant influences on what would become Hoodoo — their systems of divination, herbal knowledge, and reverence for spirit carried directly into Black American spiritual practice.

Kongo-Angola

The Kongo-Angolans (9) of West Central Africa left an equally deep mark. The Kongo cosmogram — a cross marking the boundary between the living and the spirit world — echoes throughout Hoodoo ritual to this day.

Bambara

The Bambara of Senegambia (1) held a spiritual worldview built on intimacy between the earth, the spirit world, and human beings. They were especially noted for their use of amulets and charms — objects of intercession and divination that carried protective and revealing power.

Shared Foundations

Every ethnic group carried its own distinct traditions — yet across the continent, certain spiritual foundations recur. The Akan (4) and the Igbo (7), for example, both hold a deep reverence for earth mothers, a belief that unites people to land and to ancestors in the same breath.

Where Captives Came From

The import of particular ethnic groups varied over time. This was largely due to slaveholder preferences and local ecology. For example, it is theorized that planters in South Carolina preferred Africans from the Windward Coast (Sierra Leone) and Senegambia because of their knowledge of rice cultivation. Rice became a principal crop in the region, and after 1700, the Carolina colony increasingly acquired direct shipments of these particular ethnic groups from Africa.

These patterns shaped more than the fields. The same climate and crops that made a region valuable for a particular agricultural skill also determined what plants were available for healing and ritual, how time was marked by planting and harvest seasons, and even how people dressed. And where one or two ethnic groups came to dominate a region during a given period — even as traditions melded across groups — their particular practices left a deeper imprint on what would take root locally as Hoodoo.

Tracing Documented Connections Across the Atlantic

Each line in this diagram traces a documented route — a specific African region connected to a specific American colony. West Central Africa and St. Helena formed the largest recorded source region, while South Carolina and Virginia received the greatest number of documented captives.

These routes were not evenly distributed. Colonies drew disproportionately from different African regions, shaped by the same forces already at work — the crops a colony grew, the skills its planters sought, the ports it traded through most often. The result was not one uniform culture taking root in North America, but many regional variations, each shaped by which African peoples arrived there in the greatest numbers, and what knowledge they carried with them.

Regional Traditions Take Shape

By the early 19th century, Hoodoo had taken on distinct regional characteristics. Climate, crop ecology, the structure of the local slave economy, and the size and density of plantation communities all shaped which traditions took deepest root — as did the particular skills colonists sought in the people they enslaved. Interaction with Native American and European folk and religious traditions left their own mark as well.

Northeast

States: Maryland, Virginia, North Carolina, Tenneessee
Crops: Tobacco, Corn, Wheat
Notable influences: Akan people, captives from the Gold Coast (Ghana) and Bight of Biafra (coasts of Nigeria, Cameroon, Equatorial Guinea, and northern Gabon), Kongo-Angola
Significance: Colonists in this region targeted Africans with skills in ironworking, maritime expertise, and leafy plant harvesting.

Southeast

States: South Carolina, Georgia, Florida
Crops: Rice, Indigo
Notable influences: Sierra Leone, Kongo-Angola, Igbo people (Nigeria)
Significance: While multiple African ethnic groups shaped this region, Central West African (Kongo) cosmology became a dominant and enduring influence across the South. Along the Lowcountry coast, relative isolation on rice plantations and sea islands allowed Gullah Geechee culture to develop and endure with unusual continuity — preserving language, foodways, and spiritual practice more directly tied to their African origins than almost anywhere else in North America.

Southwest

States: Missouri, Texas, Louisiana, Mississippi, Alabama
Crops: Cotton, Sugarcane
Notable influences: Senegambian Mande speakers (Bambara), Yoruba, Fon, Ewe speakers (modern-day Nigeria, Benin, Ghana, and Togo), Kongo-Angola-Zaire
Significance: Represents the inland and Gulf expansion of Hoodoo. The Bambara people were so numerous in Louisiana that their contributions to the development of Hoodoo left a widely recognized cultural legacy.

Practices and Beliefs
